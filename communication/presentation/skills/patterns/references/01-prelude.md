# Prelude Patterns

_Setting up a talk before you build it: motivation, audience, getting accepted._

**Abstract Attorney** (AP) — Attendee hunts minor deviations from your published abstract/agenda, then flags them in email, tweets, or evals instead of judging content learned; a milder Hecklers subtype. Fix: if you know material well, write a comprehensive accurate abstract (lazy generic ones invite attack); be vague when affordable (required meetings, keynotes optimized for curiosity not facts); if still conceptualizing, write broad-strokes and revisit at milestones — (1) ideation first draft with ~3 planned points, (2) after outline before building, (3) after first slide draft, (4) after several run-throughs, plus again after 4th–5th delivery (calendar reminder a month or two out); also put the abstract on slide 1 so attendees read it while settling. See: Hecklers, Preroll, Triad, Crucible, Carnegie Hall.
**Big Why** (P) — Before proposing/accepting, answer "Why am I really interested in presenting?"; a strong reason drives follow-through on a public, visible commitment (organizers market you as soon as you accept). How: pick from 10 motivation categories — (1) care about technology, (2) care about audience, (3) product sales, (4) philanthropy/open source, (5) peer recognition, (6) professional recognition, (7) concept sales, (8) convincing, (9) professional growth, (10) personal fulfillment (encompasses many others) — overlaid with your field's details. Avoid: never back out as date nears; cancellations are remembered by audience and organizers with little allowance even for extenuating circumstances. See: Carnegie Hall, Crucible.
**Know Your Audience** (P) — Learn all you can about the event and crowd (experience, knowledge, age, technical level, blended vs single-skill, attitude); a brilliant talk to the wrong audience is just a bad talk, and get this wrong and nothing else can be right. How: for work talks, know the invitation list and updates; for public events mine the event website (size, registration fees signal expectations, sponsors — don't criticize top sponsors), contact other/past speakers, read last year's site; search event hashtags on Twitter/Facebook/blogs for what content people expected and which talks landed; review posted slides/recordings (topics, attire); ask organizers about attendee level, event evals, changes made, most/least popular talks, and any attendee personas. See: Emotional State, Seeding Satisfaction.
**Proposed** (P) — Self-driven route: submit abstracts to events found via search/friends/ads, await acceptance email; the way in when organizers don't know you well enough to invite and you're not required. How: understand the (almost always web-based, quirky) CFP system — file formats, deadlines, review process, logistics; typically produce a talk title, abstract, topic outline, and sometimes past-work samples (video/PDF); write a tight active-voice abstract within guidelines, describe new info and audience benefits, sell sincerely why you should present, highlight related experience/writing/past talks (linking past-talk videos or scores gives a strong edge); ~90% of conferences use web CFP. Avoid: submission implies commitment — organizers frown on accepted speakers who decline over conflicts; choose events deliberately. See: Big Why, Know Your Audience, Peer Review, Injured Outlines, Required.
**Required** (P) — Command performance you must give as part of your job or other nonoptional reason (corporate boardrooms, branded conventions, sponsored association slots); added stress and manufactured motivation, but a low-risk chance for positive notoriety, higher-profile invites, executive exposure, and reusable talk foundations. How: use Concurrent Creation — recruit colleagues for research; involve an audience representative in building/reviewing the deck to get them on your side; plant an ally in the dreaded first row via Posse for a friendly face. Avoid: don't use lolcats/humorous filler images (see Photomaniac). See: Proposed, Posse, Concurrent Creation, Crucible.
**Social Media Advertising** (P) — Exploit Twitter, Facebook, wikis, blogs, websites, mailing lists, Meetup/Lanyrd to promote your talk cheaply; can lift public-talk attendance ~50%, and paid events too (organizers rebook speakers who draw crowds). How: longest form is a web page (dedicated site down to a wiki/blog entry) with topic details, easy signup, directions, room, expectations; short-form tweets/posts/IM use an attractive tagline plus a link to that page. Avoid: raises stakes — heightens real-time Backchannel reporting of success or failure; market just short of annoying. See: Backchannel, Seeding Satisfaction.
