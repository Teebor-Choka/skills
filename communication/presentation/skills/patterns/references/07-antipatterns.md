# Performance Antipatterns

_Delivery mistakes to avoid on stage — a pre-flight "do-not" checklist._

**Hiccup Words** (AP) — Involuntary filler nonwords (um, ah, oh) or overused real words (actually, frankly) pepper your talk; distracting to audience, hard for you to self-detect, amplifies perceived lack of knowledge. Fix: Record or have a friend count them to find them, then practice removing. Embrace silent pauses — dead air isn't fatal, no one interrupts because you paused. When stuck, a crisp "I don't know" / "I'll cover that later" or step up an abstraction level beats stammering. See: Breathing Room, Carnegie Hall.

**Going Meta** (AP) — Talking about the talk itself (built it last night, wrong slot length, clicker trouble, not fully prepared) — eats precious time, enlightens no one, and reveals problems the audience wouldn't have noticed. Fix: Never mention mechanics or resolved last-minute stressors; let the talk speak for itself. Prepare diligently, carry backups, build slack so cuts are invisible. See: Breathing Room, Narrative Arc, Carnegie Hall, Know Your Audience.

**Disowning Your Topic** (AP) — Misreading audience reaction mid-talk (mistaking quiet for mastery) and making damaging on-the-fly changes — usually speeding up — which worsens comprehension and quiets them further. Fix: Own your topic; don't over-hype nor condescend. Gauge level in advance, deliver at your practiced pace with confidence, accelerate only if you genuinely know the audience already knows it. See: Crucible, Know Your Audience.

**Tower of Babble** (AP) — Slinging specialized jargon, TLAs, and technospeak above the audience's level — comprehensible to you, nonsense to laypeople or adjacent specialists; using it to flaunt intellect backfires as they blame you. Fix: Learn audience composition and pitch technical speech to it; speak narrow argot only to colleagues, accessible terms otherwise. With translators, enunciate acronyms and slow around complex terms. See: Know Your Audience, Disowning Your Topic.

**Negative Ignorance** (AP) — Gauging familiarity by asking "Who here is NOT familiar with X?" (or the positive flip) — people won't admit ignorance before peers, so feedback is untrustworthy and you misjudge the room. Fix: Don't ask; ascertain level in advance by other means. If unsure, just deliver a 30–60s summary (or a Talklet) — knowers nod along, marginals gain context, novices get a foundation. Avoid rhetorical questions too. See: Know Your Audience, Lightning Talk, Talklet.

**Bunker** (AP) — Hiding behind podium, lectern, desk, or a raised stage/moat — kills personal connection and trust, encourages passive viewing; static presenters give static talks. Fix: Step out and walk; engage the audience directly. Gentle wandering beats hiding (don't overdo it). Stage separation is acceptable only in formal settings like keynotes. See: Carnegie Hall, Know Your Audience.

**Laser Weapons** (AP) — Leaning on a laser pointer as a crutch — causes motion sickness, invisible from the back, highlight vanishes when you release the button, and often masks slides too dense to read. Fix: Use the presentation software's built-in highlighting instead; prefer Traveling Highlights, which bakes emphasis into the talk. For rare, minimal, thoughtful use, see Lightsaber. See: Traveling Highlights, Lightsaber, Takahashi.

**Hecklers** (AP) — Outright hecklers, time sinks (question-hoggers seeking free consulting), and technical bullies/showoffs derail the talk and ruin it for the congenial audience. Fix: Prepare, stay cool, act decisively to protect the rest. Answer briefly or take it offline; if they persist, defer to the break, then escalate — invade personal space, direct eye contact, short commands. Beat technical bullies with their own ephemera or offer them the stage; never counter abuse with abuse. Admit "I don't know" over faking it. See: Know Your Audience, Bunker, Display of High Value.

**Backchannel** (AP) — The live text/tweet/chat chatter about your talk, sometimes projected on-stage — can amplify negativity, mob mentality, and public walk-out signals. Fix: Don't ignore or fight it — engage it. Pre-write the tweets you want repeated (condense your central message to 140 chars), monitor the hashtag, respond to questions, harvest crowd-posted links. It magnifies your reach and refines your ideas. See: Seeding Satisfaction, Posse.

**Dual-Headed Monster** (AP) — Serving a live and a remote/"via satellite" audience simultaneously — technical failures near-guaranteed and the two get incompatible experiences; the remote side is always diminished, diluting both. Fix: If unavoidable, allow extra setup time, practice on the actual tools, check in with the remote room, provide a plan B and fallback comms, watch platform (Mac/PC) and bandwidth issues, and stay calm when things break. See: Going Meta, Slideuments.

**Lipstick on a Pig** (AP) — Disguising weak content and thin insight with attractive slides and special effects — earns a good initial reception but near-zero lasting impact; audiences see through the facade. Fix: Fix the content, not the makeup. Get the structural skeleton (clear structure, logical flow, fresh insight) right before adding polish; budget ~an hour of prep per presentation minute. See: Photomaniac.
